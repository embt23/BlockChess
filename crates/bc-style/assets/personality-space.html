BlockChess · bc-style · episodes 05–09 ·

Personality Space

Four players, built to differ, played 120 games against each other. Nobody told the mathematics who they were. It found the axes on its own by factorising the corpus, and the games separated anyway — which is the whole claim this project rests on: that how you play is a thing that can be measured, committed to, and carried as a name.

Stage 4 · project

Where everyone stands

Every point is one game, seen from one player's side — 240 in all. Position is that game's coordinates on the first two discovered axes. Nothing here is a rating: two players can sit far apart at identical strength, because the axes measure how, not how well.

    Stage 7 · chain

    How each player moved

    A link is earned by changing, not by playing. Each panel traces one player's medal chain through the same space: a line is drawn only when they drifted far enough from their last committed position to mint a new one. Grey points are everyone else's games, kept for scale. Petrosian — the only archetype rewarded for restricting his opponent — travels least and sits furthest out.

    Stage 3 · basis

    What the axes turned out to mean

    Each axis is a tension, not a quantity — a signed direction with a meaningful pole at both ends. Being at the negative end is not the absence of a style; it is the other style. Bars show how hard each of the eighteen measured features pulls in each direction. These names were not chosen in advance: they are whichever features loaded hardest once the corpus was factorised.

    Stage 6 · commit

    The medals

    A medal is a commitment to a position under a stated lens. The corpus root is inside the hash, so the same player measured against a different set of games gets a different medal — by construction. A medal quoted without its corpus is half an identity.

    PlayerAxis 0Axis 1 SpreadGamesLinks PathMedal

    Method. Eighteen features per game-side, no engine involved — the compression measures behaviour, not correctness. Columns are z-scored against the corpus, the covariance is diagonalised by Jacobi rotation, and games are projected onto the leading eigenvectors. Eigenvector signs are pinned so a medal is reproducible on any machine.

    These four players are synthetic, and deliberately so: the pipeline's central claim is only testable against personalities whose answer is known in advance. Real human games are the next measurement, and they are what will answer the question this cannot — how many bits a chess personality actually contains, and therefore how hard a medal is to forge.
